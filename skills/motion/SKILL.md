# How things move

Motion is how a film says what matters. A viewer follows whatever changes, so each move is a claim: look here, this is the point. Six judgements follow, each with its reason and a pair of small pictures. The pictures are there for the reasoning. Don't reuse their moves or their numbers; your scene has its own point, and the right move comes from it.

## 1. The subject does the moving

Ask what the viewer should see happen in this scene, then let the thing that carries that meaning change. Boxes, cards and captions arriving on screen is setting the table. It is not the meal.

- Weak: a scene about one page that gathers a whole day. The page is there from the first frame, three empty panels float up half a second apart, their rows trickle in every 0.2 s, and for the last three seconds the page drifts 5% larger. Everything arrived and nothing happened: the gathering was never shown.
- Stronger, on another subject: a scene about a bill being split. One total sits large and still. At 1.2 s it breaks into four smaller amounts that travel to four names, in 0.3 s, and stop. The split is the motion, and the panel around it never moves.

When a scene's only motion is things entering, name its event in one sentence ("the total becomes four shares") and animate that instead.

## 2. One accent in a scene

A scene has one loudest moment, and it must arrive unlike everything around it. Emphasis is a difference. When every element comes in the same way, the eye gets no ranking and reads a list.

- Weak: across a whole film the logo, the panels, each row, each caption, a thin rule and the closing line all rise 12 px while fading in over 0.8 s on one curve. Or two lines, "No this." then "No that." 0.8 s later, entering identically: a scene about taking things away, told by adding two things.
- Stronger, on another subject: a launch date. Its supporting line is already there, small and still. At 0.9 s the date cuts in at full size with no fade, and the line under it dims by a third at the same instant. One thing happened, and it was the date.

The accent can be faster, harder, larger, a removal, or the only thing that moves at all. Put it on the word or object the scene exists for, and keep the rest quiet enough that it wins.

## 3. Fast next to still

Speed is felt only against rest. A move reads as quick when it lands and then nothing moves; a hold reads as calm when something decisive came before it. One even, gentle speed from the first frame to the last is not calm. It is the absence of a decision.

- Weak: every scene fades up from black in 0.4 s, lays out soft 0.8 s entrances on a stagger, fades back to black, and the whole picture creeps in scale throughout. Nothing is ever fast and nothing is ever still.
- Stronger, on another subject: a kettle's temperature. The dial swings from 20 to 96 in 0.25 s and stops dead. Nothing moves for 1.5 s while the number is read. Then one small label appears.

Size the hold by what must be read: about three words a second, and never under a second for anything meant to be understood. If a calm film needs slow moves, give it long true stillness between them, not a constant drift.

## 4. Which words are large

Before placing words, decide which line is the scene's lead and make the gap plain at a glance: the lead around an eighth of the frame's height or more, a supporting line a third of that or smaller. Two sizes that are nearly equal read as two things competing. Inside a line, one word can carry it, through weight, italic or colour.

- Weak: three selling points, each 4% of the frame's height, centred near the bottom edge of their scenes like subtitles, and a product name at under 3% on the opening shot. The film's message sits in its smallest type. Or two lines stacked at the same size, so neither leads.
- Stronger, on another subject: over a shot of a cyclist on the right of the frame, "Forty miles" fills the empty left third in type an eighth of the height, with "before breakfast" beneath at a third of its size.

Words on footage go where the picture is calm and away from the subject, composed with the shot and not pinned to a default position. A line that must stay small should at least be the only thing changing while it is read.

## 5. When not to move

Stillness is a choice, and often the stronger one. Footage that already moves needs nothing added: a scale push on top of it is a second, weaker motion competing with the first. A frame that is being read should hold. An ending should stop.

- Weak: a shot of someone drinking coffee, given a slow zoom, a fade from black and to black, and a caption that floats up. Three motions were added to a picture that had its own. Each scene dipping through black also makes the film five slides, not one film.
- Stronger, on another subject: a potter's hands at the wheel, untouched. One line cuts in once at 1 s and stays. The last card arrives, settles in 0.3 s and holds without drift to the final frame.

Don't add motion so that a scene is "not static". Ask whether the picture is already doing the work. Scenes may simply cut to each other; a scene need not fade itself in and out.

## 6. Saying it in the plan and in the clip

In a plan, what a scene shows should say who moves, when the accent lands and what holds, in seconds. "The three panels appear one after another" plans a container entrance. "The map is empty; at 1.0 s the route draws from the port to the city in 0.4 s, then holds while the distance is read" plans an event.

In the film's conventions, the motion line gives what the clips share: the motion's character (brisk or unhurried, its curves, how long a hold is). It should not hand every clip one entrance to apply to every element, because that is how a whole film ends up with no accent.

When writing a clip, find its event and its accent before writing any animation, then check the stills against three questions:

- Did the subject change, or did things only arrive?
- Which instant is the loudest, and does it look different from the rest?
- Where is the picture completely still, and is that long enough to read?

A scene that answers all three is usually simpler than the one it replaced.
